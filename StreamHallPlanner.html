Start herenew to this tool? 60 seconds · plain-English glossary▸
This tool answers one question: can I move my hall from where it is now to where I want it, without breaking the customer's contract — and how long will it take?
  1. Describe your hall (Data Hall card): where it is, how big it is, and how fast your plant can cool, warm, dry or humidify it. Without those rates the tool can show you the physics but cannot time the move — it will say so rather than guess.
  2. Set the contract (Customer SLA card): the temperature and humidity limits you are not allowed to cross, plus the dew-point cap and how fast you're permitted to change things.
  3. Plan the move (Conditions card): set Current to what the hall reads now and Target to where you want it. The chart draws both, the badge tells you whether each one is inside the contract, and the advisory tells you the earliest honest finish time and which limit is holding you back.
  4. Check your instruments (Sensor Validation card) before you trust any of it — six ways to verify a temperature or humidity sensor against something physical, from a jar of table salt to a pot of boiling water.
  5. Size the humidifier water (Data Hall → Ventilation moisture load): once the hall is holding its Target, the water it drinks is set by the outside air the DOAS brings in, not by the room's volume. Enter the fresh-air CFM and leave the dew point blank for the worst case.
This is one of several tools — the home screen lists them all, including the CDU flow calculator. Upload your logo in the Branding card and everything re-colours to match your site; it stays on this device.
Everything here is a planning aid — verify against site instrumentation before acting.
Plain-English glossary — what the numbers mean ▸
Dry bulb (the "temperature")
What an ordinary thermometer reads. When this app says "temp", it means this.
Relative humidity (RH %)
How full the air is, as a percentage of the most water it could hold at its current temperature. The catch: warm the same air without adding water and RH falls, because warm air can hold more. Nothing about the water changed.
Humidity ratio (W, g/kg)
The actual weight of water in the air, per kilogram of dry air. Unlike RH, this only changes when water is genuinely added or removed — which is why the tool plans moves with it.
Dew point
The temperature at which that water would start condensing. Any surface colder than the dew point gets wet — cold water pipes, chilled coils, an economizer damper on a winter morning. That's why SLAs cap it.
Wet bulb
What a thermometer reads with a wet sock over it, in moving air. Evaporation cools it, so the drier the air the lower it falls. It's what a sling psychrometer measures.
Enthalpy (kJ/kg)
The total heat energy in the air — the sensible heat you can feel plus the latent heat hiding in the water vapour. Cooling equipment is really removing enthalpy.
Sensible vs latent heat, and SHR
Sensible heat changes the temperature; latent heat changes the moisture. The sensible heat ratio (SHR) is the fraction of the job that's temperature. An SHR near 1.0 means you're almost purely cooling; a lower number means the coil is doing serious dehumidification too.
ASHRAE classes A1–A4
Industry envelopes for how far IT equipment is rated to be pushed, from the tightest (A1) to the widest (A4). The badge tells you which one your point falls inside.
Site pressure
Air pressure at your elevation. It matters because the same RH holds different amounts of water at different pressures — a plan computed at sea level is wrong in Denver.
Guard band (on sensor checks)
To honestly call a sensor "in spec", its error has to be inside tolerance by more than your reference's own uncertainty. If it isn't, the tool says "too close to call" instead of guessing.

Customer SLA—▸
SLA boundary = the binding constraint at each temperature: RH-max curve vs. dew-point cap (whichever is lower) on top, RH-min vs. dew-point floor on the bottom. Leave dew-point cap blank for an RH-only box. All boundaries recompute at site pressure. ASHRAE TC 9.9 reference ramp limits: 9°F/hr (5°C/hr) where tape media is present; 36°F/hr (20°C/hr) otherwise.

Sensor Validation6 external reference methods▸
Field-check temperature and RH sensors against an independent physical reference. Six methods, each showing the true value and its uncertainty — a verdict is only as good as the reference behind it. See docs/sensor-validation.md for when to use which.
Wetted wick, ≥3 m/s airflow. The instrument reads the psychrometric wet bulb; Eq. 35's thermodynamic one differs by ~0.5 %RH, systematically.
For sites with a chilled-mirror or other dew-point meter — reference-grade when the mirror is clean. RH follows from Tdb and Tdp alone (pressure-independent).
A saturated-salt jar is an absolute reference — physical chemistry, nothing to drift or expire — and its realized accuracy is set by how well you know the chamber temperature. NaCl barely cares (−0.04 %RH/°C); Mg(NO₃)₂ moves −0.30 %RH/°C. Sealed jar, slurry with visible solids, sensor above the slurry, hours to equilibrate. Reference: Greenspan (1977), NBS.
Crushed ice + a little distilled water — slush, not floating cubes. Stir, wait 2 min, keep the probe mid-bath off the walls. Reference: 32.0 °F / 0.00 °C by definition.
Rolling boil, probe mid-water off the pot. The reference is computed from this site's pressure — water does not boil at 212 °F at altitude. Impurities and superheat limit a field check to about ±0.9 °F.
Side-by-side with a recently calibrated instrument at the same spot, both settled. The verdict band widens by the reference's own uncertainty — a comparison can never be better than the reference.
Enter readings to compute the reference value.
All methods run at site pressure — where pressure matters. Tolerances: RH ±2% / ±5% recalibrate (typical capacitive spec); temperature ±0.9 °F / ±1.8 °F. Verdicts are guard-banded: PASS means the error is inside tolerance by more than the reference's own uncertainty; when the reference's ±u straddles the limit the verdict is "too close to call" — a check can never claim more confidence than its reference has. Methods and references: docs/sensor-validation.md.
Training — Envelope Escape Roomdrill the recovery instinct▸
A plant fault hits a standard training hall (200,000 ft³ · cool 6 / warm 4 °F/hr · dehum 100 / hum 80 lb/hr · the ASHRAE A1 envelope). Commit a recovery target; the physics referee plays out four hours minute-by-minute and scores the SLA time you kept, plus a bonus for finishing genuinely settled. Two things are always true in this hall: the servers never stop heating it — an uncommanded hall drifts warm on its own — and the plant takes minutes to spin up, so committing early beats committing perfectly. Deterministic per seed: share a challenge code and compete on identical faults.
—
The referee is src/core/trainer.js: explicit 1-minute Euler steps of the moisture/temperature balance, plant rates capped and first-order-lagged (τ ≈ 8 min), a constant server heat load on any uncommanded hall, verdicts from the same SLA check the badge uses. A drill, not a simulator — real thermal mass and control-loop tuning are deliberately out of scope, and challenge codes carry the referee version so old codes are flagged rather than silently re-scored.
